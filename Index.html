Chapter at a glance

The three ideas connect, but they are not the same thing.

6.1

Automated systems

Hardware and software designed to work automatically. The central pattern is sensor → processor/controller → actuator, often with human monitoring.

6.2

Robotics

The branch of computer science concerned with the design, construction and operation of robots. Robots sense, move and are programmable.

6.3

Artificial intelligence

Computer simulation of intelligent human behaviour, including the ability to reason, learn and adapt to external stimuli.

Core distinction: Automation describes a system operating automatically; robotics focuses on robots; AI focuses on intelligent behaviour. A robot does not automatically have AI.

6.1 Automated systems

The control loop behind many real-world automated processes.

Sensor

An input device that takes readings from its surroundings and sends data to a microprocessor or computer. Analogue data may first be converted to digital form by an ADC.

Actuator

A device controlled by signals from the computer/microprocessor. It can operate motors, wheels, solenoids, pumps, valves, heaters, pistons and similar mechanisms.

Worked control example: adaptive cruise control

The driver sets a cruising speed. Lasers in the bumpers send signals; the reflected signal helps the on-board computer calculate distance to the vehicle ahead. If the distance becomes too small, actuators apply brakes and/or reduce throttle. If the distance is safe, the computer checks whether the current speed equals the set speed and adjusts the throttle if necessary.

Automated applications

Examples from the chapter, organised around sensors, processing and action.

A distributed control system (DCS) receives data from temperature, pressure, flow level, gas-escape and radiation sensors. It consults operational data/parameters and can signal actuators to operate pumps, valves or automatic shutdown. A supervisor can monitor the process remotely and override the DCS.

Why automate? Fast action, increased safety, optimum operating conditions and reduced long-term staffing costs. Risks: high setup/testing cost, unforeseen conditions, cyberattack exposure and enhanced maintenance.

Sensors monitor manufacturing and tablet-making processes. A central computer checks whether conditions remain within required parameters and sends signals to pumps, valves, heaters, stirrers or pistons. A remote operator can monitor and override the system.

Benefits: faster action, safety, optimum conditions, efficient material use, higher productivity and consistent results.

An automatic weather station sends data to a controller. Ultrasonic water-level sensors monitor irrigation channels; wireless transmitters/receivers carry the information to the controller. The controller decides whether water pumps should start or stop and actuators operate the pumps. A remote supervisor can override the controller.

Benefits: lower labour costs, efficient water control, faster response, safety and different programmed conditions for different crops. Limitations: expensive setup, high maintenance and dependence on functioning water channels.

Typical sensors include a thermometer, anemometer, hygrometer, barometer, level sensor and light sensor. A microprocessor reads and processes the sensor data, with values stored in a central database. A tipping-bucket rain gauge can use an actuator to tip collected water at a predetermined interval.

Gaming can use accelerometers to detect acceleration/deceleration and tilt, and proximity sensors in touch pads. Automated lighting can use light sensors, motion/infrared sensors and a microprocessor to control lights and fountain pumps. Benefits include automatic control, reduced energy use and longer bulb life; disadvantages include setup and maintenance cost and, for wireless systems, possible reliability issues.

In the pharmaceutical laboratory example, level sensors and a colorimeter send readings to a microprocessor. The microprocessor controls an actuator operating a tap so that one chemical is added automatically. The process can provide repeatable results, faster parallel experiments and reduced exposure to hazardous chemicals.

6.2 Robotics

Robots combine sensing, movement and programmable control.

1 · Sense

Sensors such as light, pressure, temperature and acoustic sensors gather information about the environment. Sensor data is sent to a microprocessor or computer.

2 · Move

Wheels, cogs, pistons, gears, motors, hydraulic components and actuators allow turning, twisting, moving, gripping or lifting. End-effectors provide task-specific attachments.

3 · Programmable

A controller acts as the robot's “brain”. It uses programmed instructions and information from sensors/cameras to determine actions.

Important: Robotics and AI are not identical. Many robots perform repetitive tasks and do not possess artificial intelligence. Software bots such as search-engine WebCrawlers and chatbots are not physical robots under the chapter's definition.

Autonomous vehicle snapshot

Autonomous cars use sensors, cameras, actuators and microprocessors. Cameras provide visual data; radar and ultrasonics can help construct a 3D image of surroundings. Actuators can change gear, apply brakes and turn the steering wheel. Autonomous trains can additionally use LiDaR, proximity sensors, cameras and GPS.

6.3 Artificial intelligence (AI)

From simulated human behaviour to systems that reason, learn and adapt.

Definition

Artificial intelligence is a branch of computer science dealing with the simulation of intelligent human behaviour by a computer. The chapter relates this to cognitive functions such as acquiring knowledge and understanding through thought, experience and the five senses, with benchmarks including reasoning, speech and sight.

Narrow AI

One specific task

A machine has superior performance to a human when doing one specific task.

General AI

Human-like performance

A machine is similar, rather than superior, in performance to a human doing a specific task.

Strong AI

Many tasks

A machine has superior performance to a human in many tasks.

AI characteristics

Reasoning

Drawing reasoned conclusions from given data or situations.

Learning

Using experience/data to improve future performance.

Adaptation

Responding to external stimuli and new data; AI can identify patterns and make predictions.

Expert systems

AI that mimics human expert knowledge and decision-making.

Knowledge base

A repository of facts, represented as objects and attributes. Example categories include animals, illnesses or other areas of expertise.

Rules base

A set of inference rules used by the inference engine to draw conclusions, often using IF … THEN logic.

Inference engine

The main processing element. It examines the knowledge base for matches and asks questions based on earlier responses.

Explanation system

Explains the reasoning behind conclusions and recommendations. The chapter notes this is not explicitly covered in the syllabus, but it appears in the typical structure.

Example reasoning: IF continent = “South America” AND language = “Portuguese” THEN country = “Brazil”. The rules base provides the inference rule; the inference engine applies it to information supplied by the user.

Expert-system strengths and limitations

Advantages

  • High level of expertise and accuracy
  • Consistent results
  • Stores large amounts of facts
  • Traceable logical solutions/diagnostics
  • Fast response times
  • Can provide probability of a suggested solution

Disadvantages

  • Users need considerable training
  • High setup and maintenance costs
  • Responses can be “cold” in some situations
  • Only as good as the facts entered
  • Users may wrongly assume the system is infallible

Machine learning & deep learning

Learning from examples rather than writing a separate rule for every new case.

Machine learning

Machine learning is a subset of AI in which algorithms are trained and learn from past experiences and examples. The chapter describes training computers with sample data so they can make predictions about new unseen data without being specifically programmed for every new case.

Deep learning extension in the chapter

Deep learning uses layered artificial neural networks: an input layer, hidden layer(s) and an output layer. The hidden layers process input data into information sent towards the output. The chapter uses complex visual processing as an example, including recognition of objects from patterns in pixel data.

Key dependency: AI can access large amounts of data and find patterns that humans might miss, but AI is dependent on the data used to train it.

Self-reflection questions — with answers

Conceptual prompts for checking understanding.

1. Why is a sensor not enough to make an automated system work?

Answer: A sensor only gathers input data. The microprocessor/computer must process that data and determine an action, and actuators are needed to carry out physical actions where required.

2. In a nuclear power station, why can a human supervisor still be present if the system is fully automated?

Answer: The supervisor can monitor the process and override the DCS if necessary. This is important because conditions not considered during testing may occur and computerised systems can also face security risks.

3. What is the difference between an actuator and a sensor?

Answer: A sensor is an input device that gathers information from the surroundings. An actuator receives a control signal and produces a physical action such as moving a motor, operating a valve or applying a brake.

4. Why is an automated weather station useful in a remote region?

Answer: It can automatically gather and store weather data without requiring people to be physically present. It can use a range of sensors and transmit/process the data regularly.

5. Give the three characteristics that allow a device to be called a robot in this chapter.

Answer: It can sense its surroundings, it has a degree of movement, and it is programmable through a controller.

6. Why is a robot not necessarily an AI system?

Answer: Many robots perform programmed, repetitive tasks. AI is concerned with simulating intelligent behaviour, including reasoning, learning and adaptation. A robot may have sensors and movement without having adaptive AI.

7. What is the difference between an independent and a dependent robot?

Answer: An independent/autonomous robot has no direct human control and can carry out the human activity on its own. A dependent robot has a human interface and supplements rather than totally replaces human activity.

8. How does an autonomous car use sensors, a processor and actuators together?

Answer: Cameras, radar and ultrasonics collect information about the surroundings. The microprocessor processes the data and determines an action. Actuators then perform actions such as changing gear, applying brakes or turning the steering wheel.

9. What makes an expert system different from a general database?

Answer: An expert system uses a knowledge base together with inference rules and an inference engine to reason from user-provided information and reach conclusions or recommendations.

10. What is the role of the inference engine?

Answer: It is the main processing element. It asks questions, examines the knowledge base for matching information and applies the inference rules to draw conclusions.

11. Why can an expert system produce an incorrect conclusion?

Answer: The system is only as good as the information/facts entered into it. Incorrect, incomplete or unsuitable knowledge can therefore lead to an incorrect result.

12. What is machine learning in one sentence?

Answer: It is a subset of AI in which algorithms are trained using sample data and learn from past experience/examples so they can make predictions about new unseen data.

13. Why is testing important in a machine-learning system?

Answer: Known labelled data can be used to check whether the model gives accurate responses. If the output is not sufficiently accurate, the model can be refined until satisfactory results are obtained.

14. A factory robot is accurate but cannot handle a cracked windscreen during fitting. What does this illustrate?

Answer: Robots are well suited to standard, repetitive tasks, but they can have difficulty with non-standard tasks and variable circumstances that require flexible human judgement.

Chapter summary

The essential ideas to retain.

Automated systems

  • Sensor input is processed by a microprocessor/computer.
  • Actuators carry out physical actions.
  • Automation is used in industry, transport, agriculture, weather, gaming, lighting and science.
  • Advantages and disadvantages depend on the scenario.

Robotics

  • Robotics covers design, construction and operation of robots.
  • Robots sense, move and are programmable.
  • Robots may be independent/autonomous or dependent.
  • Many robots do not have AI.

AI

  • AI simulates intelligent human behaviour.
  • Key characteristics: reasoning, learning and adaptation.
  • The chapter distinguishes narrow, general and strong AI.
  • Examples include smart devices, chatbots, autonomous cars and facial-expression recognition.

AI systems

  • Expert systems use a knowledge base, rules base and inference engine.
  • Machine learning trains algorithms from data and past examples.
  • Deep learning uses layered artificial neural networks as an extension topic.
  • AI depends on the data used to train it.
Exam-thinking anchor: When a question describes a new automated or robotic scenario, identify what is sensed → what the processor decides → what the actuator does, then evaluate scenario-specific advantages and disadvantages.
